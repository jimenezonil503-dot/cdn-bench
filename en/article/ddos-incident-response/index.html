DDoS Incident Response: Triage, Containment and Recovery

During a DDoS incident, establish impact and a timeline, then mitigate at the affected layer with evidence, escalation and reversible changes.

CDN Bench editorial · 2026-10-14 · Updated 2026-10-147 min read
In this article
  1. Restore the essential service first
  2. Preserve correlated records before dashboards alone
  3. Locate pressure with three question groups
  4. Escalate with actionable evidence
  5. Keep temporary controls narrow and reversible
  6. Coordinate changes to avoid conflicting actions
  7. Rollback must retain protection
  8. Verify recovery and close the incident

Restore the essential service first

Do not close everything or change every rule merely because traffic rises. Establish affected users/workflows and start time, assign a coordinator and keep a shared timeline. Retain alternative explanations such as releases, configuration faults or legitimate growth until evidence supports an attack.

CISA guidance informs this defensive workflow, checked against public material on 14 October 2026. Prefer authorized, reversible controls with clear effects.

Preserve correlated records before dashboards alone

Record timezone, assets, audience, errors, request identifiers, cache state and origin timing. Share redacted samples without credentials or personal data. Peak screenshots cannot establish duration or causal improvement.

Avoid unbounded emergency logging. Use affordable sampling with documented scope. Log every change, owner, expected effect and rollback so falling attack intensity is not mistaken for proof of the last rule.

Locate pressure with three question groups

Application limits cannot repair saturated upstream ingress, while more network capacity may not fix database queues. Check multiple layers concurrently.

Escalate with actionable evidence

Provide assets, timing, regions, impact, changes and redacted identifiers. Ask whether mitigation is active, routes changed and approvals are needed. Record upgrade scope and effective time to prevent conflicting requests.

Use Cloudflare scope and AWS protocol guidance appropriately. For CDN5 or other bundles, escalate against the actual order—not brand-wide assumptions.

Keep temporary controls narrow and reversible

Protect critical routes with scoped controls and observe legitimate completion. Defer expensive nonessential work with owner agreement, and cache only verified shareable content.

Avoid indiscriminate shared-IP blocks or browser challenges for APIs. Rate rules help separate scope, counters and action. Fix local false positives without removing the whole layer, and bound client retries.

Coordinate changes to avoid conflicting actions

Maintain shared impact, active actions and reassessment timing. Coordinate routing, purges and restarts so multiple operators do not overwrite the same controls.

Communicate verified impact and recovery, avoiding unsupported attacker or scale claims. Restrict sensitive incident records.

Rollback must retain protection

Do not use bare-origin DNS as a universal recovery shortcut. Verify standby TLS, policies, allowlists, capacity and state. DNS caches and existing sessions create overlap; validate both paths and user results.

Restrict exposed origins while preserving checks. Stateful recovery needs idempotency and consistency. See website recovery and CC triage.

Verify recovery and close the incident

Recovery means completed workflows, acceptable segmented errors/tails and drained queues—not just HTTP 200. Relax controls gradually, watching reconnection and backlogs, with owners for residual issues.

Archive timelines/configurations, reconcile retries/refill/temporary costs, revoke exceptions and close exposed paths. Assign and verify improvements to detection, coordination, policies and architecture before closure.
